E-E-A-T is a useful way to review whether content demonstrates **experience, expertise, authoritativeness, and trust**. It is not a single Google ranking score, and it does not decide whether an AI platform will cite your business.

Google explains that E-E-A-T is a framework used in its search quality rater guidelines. The raters help Google evaluate whether its systems are producing useful results, but their ratings do not directly determine an individual page's ranking. Google also says E-E-A-T itself is not a specific ranking factor. Trust is the most important part of the framework.

For a small business, the practical question is simpler: **can a customer or search system verify who you are, what you know, what you have done, and whether your claims are dependable?**

## The four parts in plain language

| Element | The question a reader is asking | Useful evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Experience | Have you actually done this work? | Original photos, project details, lessons, process notes, limitations |
| Expertise | Do you have suitable knowledge for this topic? | Named authors, relevant qualifications, work history, accurate explanations |
| Authoritativeness | Are you recognised in this field or community? | Relevant mentions, associations, references, links, client or industry context |
| Trust | Is the page and business honest, accurate, and accountable? | Contact details, policies, sources, secure transactions, corrections, consistent claims |

The type and strength of evidence should match the claim. A personal account may be enough for "what we learned redesigning this service page." Legal, tax, medical, safety, or financial guidance needs qualified expertise and authoritative sources.

## What Google actually asks content owners to consider

Google's [people-first content guidance](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content) suggests reviewing the **Who, How, and Why** behind content:

- **Who created it?** Give readers a byline and useful information about the author where they would reasonably expect it.
- **How was it created?** Explain the method when that helps a reader assess the work, especially for comparisons, tests, reviews, or AI-assisted material.
- **Why was it created?** The primary purpose should be to help the intended audience, not merely to attract search visits.

This is more useful than adding a generic author biography to every page. The evidence should sit close to the claim it supports.

## A practical trust checklist for an Auckland business

### 1. Identify the real people behind the business

Use real names on the About page and on content where authorship matters. Explain each person's role and relevant experience without inflating credentials. Link to a professional profile or recognised registration where appropriate.

An anonymous "team of experts" is harder to evaluate than a named owner who explains what they do and how long they have done it.

### 2. Show first-hand work

Generic advice is easy to reproduce. First-hand evidence is not. Useful examples include:

- a case study with the client's permission
- original screenshots or project photographs
- the initial problem and constraints
- what was changed and why
- a result that can be measured or clearly qualified
- what did not work, or what you would do differently

Kiwi Web Design's own case-study pages are strongest when they show the actual client website and explain the design or conversion problem addressed. The evidence becomes weaker when a page relies on a stock image or a broad claim such as "we deliver results" without showing the work.

### 3. Make important claims checkable

Separate three kinds of statement:

- **Fact:** link to the primary source where possible.
- **First-hand observation:** say what you observed and in what context.
- **Opinion or recommendation:** explain the criteria and trade-offs.

For example, "Google requires every local business to add FAQ schema" would need an official Google source and is not accurate as a blanket claim. "We often find outdated phone numbers in client schema audits" is a practice-based observation and should be labelled that way.

### 4. Keep business details consistent

Check the business name, phone, address or service area, email, opening hours, and website across the site's visible pages, structured data, Google Business Profile, and major profiles.

Consistency does not prove quality, but conflicting details create avoidable uncertainty for customers and machines.

### 5. Use genuine, current reviews

Show reviews with permission and enough context to understand what service was provided. Do not invent, selectively rewrite, or mark up reviews that are not visible. If you display an aggregate rating, keep the rating and count current and make its source clear.

### 6. Be transparent about commercial intent

If you include your own company in a comparison, disclose it prominently. If a link is sponsored or earns a commission, say so. Explain how a shortlist was assembled and when it was checked.

Transparency does not weaken a commercial page. It gives the reader the information needed to judge it fairly.

### 7. Publish the policies customers need

Depending on the business, this can include privacy, returns, cancellation, delivery, warranty, complaints, terms, and contact information. Policies should reflect the actual operation rather than a template copied from another country.

### 8. Maintain and correct the content

Add a meaningful last-updated date when facts have been rechecked. Remove outdated prices and unsupported statistics. When an important error is found, correct it plainly rather than leaving contradictory versions across the site.

## What E-E-A-T does not mean

E-E-A-T is often turned into claims Google does not make. It does not mean:

- adding an author box will automatically improve rankings
- a backlink from any website proves authority
- schema markup creates trust by itself
- AI-written content is automatically poor or automatically good
- a high review count overrides inaccurate or unhelpful content
- an AI assistant must cite the business with the most visible trust signals

Google's [guidance on generative AI content](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/using-gen-ai-content) focuses on accuracy, quality, and relevance. The production tool is less important than whether the final page is genuinely useful and responsibly reviewed.

## A 30-minute website review

Start with the five pages most likely to influence a customer's decision: Home, About, the primary service page, Contact, and one strong case study or article.

For each page, ask:

1. Is it clear who is responsible for this claim or service?
2. Is there first-hand evidence that could not be copied from a generic article?
3. Are factual claims sourced or carefully qualified?
4. Do the contact and business details agree everywhere?
5. Is anything outdated, exaggerated, or difficult to verify?
6. Does the page help the intended customer make a decision?

Fixing one unsupported claim or adding one real project example can be more valuable than publishing another broad article about the same topic.

## The useful conclusion

Do not treat E-E-A-T as a score to optimise. Treat it as an editorial test: **is this page honest about who made it, grounded in appropriate experience, supported by suitable evidence, and safe for a reader to rely on?**

That standard helps traditional search, AI-assisted discovery, and conversion because it improves the page itself. It still cannot guarantee a ranking or citation.
